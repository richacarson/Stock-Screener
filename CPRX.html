Company Profile
Healthcare ·Biotechnology ·NASDAQ ·United States
Catalyst Pharmaceuticals is a commercial-stage biopharmaceutical company focused on rare diseases. Its lead product FIRDAPSE (amifampridine) is the only FDA-approved treatment for Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS), supplemented by AGAMREE (vamorolone) for Duchenne muscular dystrophy and FYCOMPA (perampanel) for epilepsy. The company was acquired by Italy's Angelini Pharma and delisted from NASDAQ on July 15, 2026. Previously headquartered in Coral Gables, Florida.
200 Employees ·www.catalystpharma.com

Finite vs Infinite Game — Sinek (25%)
Mindset: FINITE Overall: 4 /10
Catalyst Pharmaceuticals plays a predominantly finite game. While the rare disease focus provides a genuine connection to patient impact, the company's actions — pricing FIRDAPSE at $375K, suing the FDA to block a competitor's approval, and settling with Teva to delay generics — reveal a business prioritizing monopoly protection and revenue maximization over its stated mission of transformative rare disease treatment. The Angelini Pharma acquisition ends the company's independent existence.

Investment Thesis
Catalyst Pharmaceuticals built an exceptional rare disease franchise: FIRDAPSE (the only FDA-approved LEMS treatment) generated $98.9M in Q1 2026 (up 18.1% YoY), AGAMREE grew 66.6% to $36.7M, and the combined portfolio produced record FY2025 revenue of $589M with 37.1% profit margin and zero debt [1][2]. The company's financial profile was remarkable — 24.5% ROE, 37.7% 5-year revenue CAGR, $755.9M cash, PEG of 1.26 — earned through monopoly rare disease positions with exclusivity extending to 2035. The Angelini Pharma acquisition at $31.49/share captured this value for shareholders.
The bear case was rendered moot by the Angelini Pharma acquisition (delisted July 15, 2026), but the structural risks remain relevant for understanding the business. FYCOMPA revenue declined 61.3% in Q1 2026 after loss of exclusivity, previewing the eventual fate of all pharmaceutical franchises when patent protection expires [1]. The FIRDAPSE pricing controversy ($375K annual list price) and the Teva settlement delaying generics until 2035 illustrated the regulatory and reputational risks of rare disease pricing models. More fundamentally, Catalyst's pipeline was thin — no new drug candidates were in development, and the company relied on acquisitions of approved drugs rather than de novo discovery, limiting long-term innovation capacity. The 200-employee company lacked the scale and infrastructure for sustainable independent growth, which ultimately led to the Angelini sale.
