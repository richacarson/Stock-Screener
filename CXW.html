Company Profile
Industrials ·Specialty Business Services ·NYSE ·United States
CoreCivic, Inc. is the largest private owner of correctional and detention facilities in the United States, operating approximately 72 facilities with roughly 76,000 beds. The company provides corrections, detention, and residential reentry services to federal, state, and local government agencies. CoreCivic operates through three segments: CoreCivic Safety (corrections and detention), CoreCivic Community (residential reentry centers), and CoreCivic Properties (real estate solutions for government partners). The company's largest federal customer is U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), whose revenue nearly doubled in Q1 2026. New CEO Patrick Swindle succeeded long-tenured Damon Hininger (2009-2025) effective January 1, 2026. Headquartered in Brentwood, Tennessee.

Investment Thesis
CoreCivic is delivering exceptional near-term financial performance driven by a favorable policy environment: Q1 2026 revenue surged 25.8% to $614.7M and Q2 grew 27.3% to $684.9M, with ICE revenue nearly doubling as four previously idled facilities were reactivated [1]. Full-year 2026 guidance projects adjusted EBITDA of $453.8-$461.8M and net income of $147.8-$157.8M. The company has monetized assets aggressively, selling two facilities to DHS for $734M in August 2026. At a forward P/E of 13.25, the stock is attractively valued relative to earnings growth of 63% YoY. EPS growth of 9.7% compounded over five years demonstrates consistent earnings expansion even through prior policy headwinds. The operational leverage is significant — reactivating idle facilities generates revenue on existing assets with minimal incremental capital expenditure.
The bear case is dominated by policy risk and ESG concerns: CoreCivic's business model is entirely dependent on government policy decisions regarding immigration enforcement, incarceration, and private corrections contracting [1]. Any change in political administration, executive order, or legislative action could rapidly reverse ICE demand — the Biden administration's 2021 executive order to phase out private prisons demonstrates this policy vulnerability, and the current growth cycle could reverse equally fast. Sustained insider selling during record earnings growth raises concerns that leadership views the current environment as a temporary window rather than a durable trend [3]. The suspended dividend removes a capital return mechanism and may signal management uncertainty about future cash flows. ESG and governance headwinds limit the investor base — many institutional investors, pension funds, and ESG-focused allocators exclude private prison operators. The 5.53% profit margin leaves limited cushion for cost increases, and the labor-intensive business model faces wage pressure in a tight correctional officer labor market.
Key Catalysts
  1. Continued ICE facility demand and potential additional government contracts as immigration enforcement policies drive occupancy rates above historical levels across CoreCivic's 76,000-bed portfolio
  2. Additional asset monetization through facility sales to government agencies at premium valuations, following the $734M DHS transaction model, unlocking embedded real estate value
  3. Dividend reinstatement using strong free cash flow ($453-462M adjusted EBITDA guidance), which would attract income-focused investors and signal management confidence in sustained earnings
Key Risks
  1. Policy reversal risk — CoreCivic's revenue surge is entirely policy-driven; any shift in immigration enforcement, executive orders to phase out private corrections, or reduced federal detention budgets would rapidly compress occupancy and earnings
  2. Sustained insider selling during record earnings growth signals that leadership may view the current policy-driven tailwind as temporary rather than structural, undermining the long-term investment case
  3. ESG exclusion from institutional portfolios — many pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, and ESG-focused allocators categorically exclude private prison operators, limiting the stock's potential investor base and creating a valuation ceiling
