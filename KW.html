Company Profile
Real Estate ·Real Estate Services ·NYSE ·United States
Kennedy-Wilson Holdings, Inc. was a global real estate investment company with $36 billion in assets under management, focusing on multifamily rental housing, industrial properties, and real estate debt origination. Founded in 1977 by CEO William McMorrow. The company completed a take-private transaction on June 16, 2026, acquired by a consortium led by McMorrow and Fairfax Financial Holdings Limited at $10.90 per share in an all-cash transaction valued at US$1.65 billion. KW shares are no longer publicly traded.

Investment Thesis
Kennedy-Wilson completed its take-private transaction on June 16, 2026 at $10.90 per share, making this effectively a closed investment thesis for public market investors [4]. The $36B AUM platform anchored by multifamily housing and industrial properties represents durable real estate value, and the Toll Brothers Apartment Living acquisition ($334M for $5B AUM) demonstrates management's conviction in the growth trajectory [1]. Q1 2026 marked a return to profitability with $24.6M net income, investment management fees growing 11% YoY, and fair value gains of $45M in the co-investment portfolio [1]. McMorrow and Fairfax's willingness to invest $1.65B in personal and institutional capital signals deep long-term conviction. The Inspire Impact Score of 54 (ALIGNED) with 13 positive attributions reflects genuine ESG commitment across 9.2M sq ft of sustainably certified space [3].
For any investors who held KW through the take-private, the $10.90 buyout price raises concerns about fair value. The management consortium acquired at near-52-week lows following years of financial deterioration — D/E of 315%, cumulative 2023-2024 net losses of $336M, and consecutive dividend cuts from $0.96 to $0.48 annually [5]. The buyout price arguably undervalues the $36B AUM platform and the Q1 2026 profitability inflection. Revenue growth of 16.3% and profit margin of 15.9% in Q1 2026 suggest the turnaround was gaining momentum precisely when McMorrow chose to take the company private, transferring upside from public shareholders to the insider consortium. The 315% D/E ratio remains dangerously high in a higher-rate environment, and the private structure eliminates the transparency that public reporting provided.
