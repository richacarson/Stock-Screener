Company Profile
Industrials ·Aerospace & Defense ·NYSE ·United States
Voyager Technologies (formerly Voyager Space Holdings, renamed February 2025) is a defense technology and space solutions company operating through three segments: Defense & National Security (solid propulsion, signal intelligence, AI edge computing), Space Solutions (in-space propulsion, Bishop Airlock on ISS, space science), and Starlab Space Stations (commercial ISS successor). Led by CEO and Co-Founder Dylan Taylor, Voyager raised its full-year 2026 revenue guidance to $275-305M (66-84% YoY growth) after record Q2 revenue of $52.7M (+51% sequentially). The company completed its transformational acquisition of Astrobotic, won a $298M NASA lunar lander contract, and was awarded the seventh ISS Private Astronaut Mission. Record backlog of $335.5M with $113M Q2 bookings (+200% YoY) provides strong revenue visibility into 2027. Headquartered in Denver, market cap approximately $2.2B.

Investment Thesis
Voyager Technologies is a high-conviction bet on the commercial space and defense technology convergence. The company raised FY2026 revenue guidance to $275-305M (66-84% YoY growth) after record Q2 revenue of $52.7M (+51% sequentially), with record backlog of $335.5M and $113M in Q2 bookings (+200% YoY) providing strong visibility into 2027 [1]. The Astrobotic acquisition creates an integrated space infrastructure platform spanning propulsion, lunar landers, space stations (Starlab as ISS successor), and defense electronics. A $298M NASA contract for two lunar landers validates the technology platform at the highest level. At $2.2B market cap with $275-305M revenue guidance, the stock trades at roughly 7-8x forward revenue — a reasonable multiple for a 66-84% growth company building long-duration space infrastructure assets [3].
The bear case centers on deep unprofitability, execution risk, and dilution. With profit margin of -78.6% and ROE of -30.4%, Voyager is burning substantial cash while pursuing its ambitious growth agenda — the company lost approximately $1.60 per share (forward EPS) with no clear path to near-term profitability [3]. D/E of 130.4% reflects meaningful leverage for a pre-profit company, and continued capital-intensive investment in Starlab, lunar landers, and defense programs will likely require additional equity or debt financing, diluting existing shareholders. The company's growth has been primarily acquisition-driven (Astrobotic, The Spaceship Company), creating integration risk and raising questions about organic revenue generation capability. The commercial space station market is unproven — Starlab must compete with Axiom Space's more advanced program, and the ISS decommission timeline remains uncertain. Government funding risk is real — NASA's budget priorities can shift with administrations, and defense contracts carry political uncertainty. The stock at $36.60 is above its 50-day SMA of $33.26 on momentum, but a growth deceleration or funding gap could trigger a sharp correction.
